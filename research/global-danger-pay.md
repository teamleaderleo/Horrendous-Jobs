# Global Danger-to-Pay Cabinet

*Research note, observed 2026-09-08.*

The ordinary Horrendous Jobs leaderboard scores public job postings. This note looks at a different beast: occupations and labor arrangements where physical danger, miserable conditions, weak compensation, wage theft, coercion, or inability to leave pile on top of each other.

This is a research layer beside the specimen index. The tiers below are editorial. They do not use the 0–10 Horrendous Score, and they should not be copied into the posting leaderboard without a specific public posting and its own evidence.

There is no clean global unit for “money-to-danger-and-shittiness ratio.” A taka wage in Chattogram, a yuan wage in Kachin State, debt-bonded brick work in Punjab, and an undocumented gold miner’s share in South Africa are economically different things. The useful comparison is the whole worker bargain:

- chance of death or permanent injury;
- toxic or chronic exposure;
- hours and physical demands;
- actual pay and whether it arrives;
- recruitment debt or wage deductions;
- freedom to quit and leave the worksite.

Once debt bondage or forced labor enters the picture, the compensation side of the ratio can approach zero.

## Cabinet

| Tier | Work / place | Compensation reality | Why the bargain is horrific |
|---|---|---|---|
| **S+** | **Shipbreaking, Chattogram, Bangladesh** | Human Rights Watch found cutters reporting an average **11,564 BDT/month** in its 2023 investigation, with helpers as low as **5,200 BDT/month**. In December 2025, workers said the **16,000 BDT** minimum still was not properly implemented and some workers were doing 12-hour days. | Falling steel, explosions, molten metal, asbestos and toxic confined spaces. On **14 August 2026**, nine workers and employees died after toxic-gas exposure inside an LNG carrier being dismantled. |
| **S+** | **Brick kilns, Pakistan** | Debt and deductions can swallow the wage. The U.S. Department of Labor describes bonded families living at kilns, with some children receiving little or no payment after debt deductions. | Families can be tied to inherited debt, prohibited from leaving, and forced to produce **1,000+ bricks per day** under threats of violence or death. Children work alongside adults, often without basic protective equipment. |
| **S+** | **Hazardous sewer and septic-tank cleaning, India** | Pay varies by municipality, contractor and informal arrangement, so there is no honest single wage to put here. The worker is often at the bottom of a contracting chain. | India’s government reported **332 sanitation-worker deaths from January 2021 through June 2026** due to hazardous sewer and septic-tank cleaning. The work can involve confined-space gases that incapacitate or kill before a worker can escape. Hazardous manual cleaning is prohibited by law. |
| **S** | **Migrant fishing and seafood work, East and Southeast Asia** | High recruitment costs, inadequate wage protection and wage abuse are recurring problems. | The ILO’s 2026 regional survey estimated **13%**, roughly one in eight migrant workers, were in forced-labor situations: working against their will and unable to leave. The same survey found long hours, serious occupational injuries and recruitment problems. At sea, “quit” can be geographically meaningless. |
| **S** | **Artisanal cobalt, copper, gold, tin, tantalum and tungsten mining, DRC** | Earnings vary heavily by mineral, site and trading chain. Some workers enter because artisanal mining can beat local alternatives; that does little to tame the hazard. | The U.S. Department of Labor documents forced child labor in gold, tin, tantalum and tungsten mining and dangerous child labor in cobalt and copper, including underground work, digging and heavy loads. Tunnel collapse, dust and conflict exposure sit inside the same economy. |
| **S** | **Rare-earth mining, Kachin State, Myanmar** | Workers interviewed in 2026 described nominal wages around **2,600–3,000 yuan/month**, sometimes delayed four or five months; one family described receiving only **1,000 yuan** while the rest remained unpaid. | Rare-earth extraction has expanded inside a conflict economy. OHCHR has documented large-scale leaching operations, pollution and links between mining revenue and armed groups. The job combines chemical exposure, weak wage reliability and armed territorial control. |
| **A** | **Migrant construction and industrial work, Saudi Arabia** | Absolute wages can be higher than the S-tier cases, but wage theft can erase the advantage. Human Rights Watch documented hundreds of workers at one subcontractor going unpaid for up to **eight months**. | Human Rights Watch documented migrant workers dying from falls, electrocution, machinery accidents and decapitation, alongside weak investigation and compensation. Extreme heat adds another occupational risk. |
| **A** | **Illegal gold mining, South Africa** | No stable public wage benchmark exists for the underground illegal economy; earnings depend on criminal supply chains and recovered gold. | At Stilfontein, a mine more than **two kilometres deep**, rescuers ultimately recovered **78 bodies** and brought out hundreds of survivors during the 2024–25 standoff. Desperation, illegal status, gang control and underground hazards turn abandoned mine shafts into an employment market. |
| **A** | **Sugarcane cutting, Nicaragua / Central America** | Piece-rate and seasonal arrangements vary. The strongest evidence here is the bodily cost rather than one comparable wage figure. | Longitudinal research on Nicaraguan cane cutters found a significant decline in kidney function over only nine weeks of harvest work. Repeated high-intensity labor in heat is a major suspected driver of Mesoamerican nephropathy. |
| **A** | **Informal e-waste recycling, Ghana and similar sites** | Informal recycling is an income source, but earnings are unstable and vary by material recovered. | Workers recover metals through dismantling, open burning, heating and sometimes acid processes. WHO describes exposure to lead, cadmium, mercury, dioxins and other toxicants; Agbogbloshie in Ghana became a prominent example of heavy contamination around informal recycling work. |

## Cabinet verdicts

**Worst pure danger for ordinary pay:** Bangladesh shipbreaking. The worker can be cutting apart a giant steel vessel around toxic residues and explosive hazards for a wage that labor groups themselves describe as inadequate.

**Worst overall employment relationship:** Pakistan brick kilns. Once inherited debt, threats and restrictions on leaving appear, “bad pay” has crossed into bondage.

**Most insane individual task:** hazardous sewer and septic-tank cleaning in India. A worker enters a confined space where invisible gas can turn a routine cleaning job into a multiple-fatality rescue attempt.

**Worst loss of exit freedom:** migrant fishing. Debt, recruitment costs and document or wage problems are ugly on land; a vessel hundreds of kilometres offshore adds a physical barrier to leaving.

**Conflict-economy award:** DRC artisanal mining and Myanmar rare-earth mining. Extraction, poverty, armed power and weak labor protection occupy the same patch of earth.

**Rich-state embarrassment:** Saudi migrant construction and industrial work. The surrounding economy is wealthy enough to buy every guardrail, harness, cooling system, payroll system and investigation it needs; documented workers still die in avoidable accidents or wait months for wages.

**South African footnote from hell:** mass formal unemployment can coexist with an underground economy where people enter abandoned gold mines kilometres below ground. A labor market can become so exclusionary that mortal peril starts looking like a route back into cash income.

## How this fits Horrendous Jobs

The existing **Danger Premium** remains the right overlay for a specific public posting. This note has a broader purpose: it gives that cabinet a global reference point.

A Toronto high-rise window-cleaning posting at CAD $18/hour can deserve a 5/5 Danger Premium because the advertised compensation is strikingly ordinary for catastrophic fall exposure. The examples above show the far end of the same idea, where safety systems, wage reliability, legal status and even the worker’s freedom to leave can all degrade together.

For future specimen hunting, especially useful categories include:

- confined-space cleaning;
- industrial demolition and shipbreaking;
- migrant construction;
- mining and quarrying;
- commercial fishing;
- hazardous-waste and e-waste processing;
- extreme-heat agricultural work;
- jobs with recruitment debt, withheld documents or wage delays.

A public posting from one of those categories should still be scored on its own terms. The global cabinet is context, not a substitute for the posting.
